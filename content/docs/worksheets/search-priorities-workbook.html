The gap this closes

The pipeline already mines demand. Nothing tells it which demand is worth money.

Three tools are live: daedalus-insights pulls Search Console and GA4 into a ranked opportunity queue, demand-scout turns queue items into content briefs, and the client pack governs what may be claimed. Every one of them ranks by opportunity — impressions, position, striking distance. None of them knows what an hour of the resulting work is worth, or which winnable queries are the wrong ones to win.

That gap has a shape, and it is already visible in Tall Karol's own published knowledge base. Fifty-one posts are live, each registered with a targetQuery — the query it was written to win. Sorted by who actually types those phrases:

Read it carefully. The practitioner posts are not waste — they are the strongest writing on the site, they earn citations, and they are why the domain has any technical authority at all. The problem is proportion and intent, against a stated goal of $80/hr now, $120/hr by April 2028. One live target query is cheapest way to host a side project. It will rank. Nothing in the current stack would have flagged that, because nothing in the current stack has been told what the studio is trying to be paid. This workbook is that missing statement — and its most valuable stage is the one that says what to refuse. The archetype: a supplement client who does not sell CBD, whose organic traffic will fill with CBD queries the moment a research tool is left to optimize freely.

Mode 1

The client fills it in

Sent ahead of kickoff. Twenty minutes. Works when the client has opinions and time — rarer than it sounds.

Mode 2

Filled from an interview, then corrected

The default. Every answer carries a provenance chip, so the client's review is a scan for the dashed ones rather than a re-read. That is the mode demonstrated on this page.

Mode 3

An onboarding portal

Selections and short fields, exported in the same ID-prefixed format. Same contract, no re-typing — the pattern already proven by the Website Planning Workbook.

The IDs are load-bearing. Answers export as M2. … line-prefixed blocks, the same contract the planning workbook keeps, so a returned workbook parses by ID into a research brief. Reword a question freely when asking it out loud; never renumber one.

M

The money

What the work is worth, and what it needs to be worth. Every later stage is ranked against this one — a query is only an opportunity if the work behind it clears the floor.

M1Stated

What does a good engagement look like — size, shape, and how long it lasts?

Sets the unit the whole plan is measured in. A plan that produces the wrong shape of lead is a failed plan even if traffic triples.

A monthly retainer, not a one-off build. Projects are the entry point and most should convert — a scoped project with a finish line, then an hours bank. Three bank sizes are already defined internally: Maintain (~10–20 hrs/mo), Build (~40–60), Embedded (~80+). Build and Embedded are the target.

Source: Karol, 2026-08-29 · strategy doc §7

M2Stated

What is the effective hourly rate today, what should it be, and by when?

The single number that ranks every opportunity later. Ask for the effective rate — what the work actually earned after overrun — not the quoted rate.

Today: around $60/hr effective across the book, and that is acceptable for current work.

  • $80/hr — the floor on all new work from now on.
  • $120/hr — the target for all new work as of April 2028.
  • A two-year phase-in. Some $80/hr clients will still be on the books in April 2028; the target governs new work only.
  • Older, cheaper clients get phased out as new retainers replace the revenue — not before.

M3Stated

Which work in flight would you not take again at the price you took it?

Finds the gap between what was sold and what was delivered. Usually names a service line to stop marketing, not a bad client.

Artist House will land closer to $55/hr against an $80 target — the UI got built past the scope that was priced. The pattern is understood and accepted: a strong artifact creates a pipeline of small high-value improvements (an $800 UI feature that takes three hours), which pulls the average back up. Acceptable at ~$60 for now, not repeatable as a pricing model.

Engagements with a delivered intelligence layer: reports that arrive on their own, dashboards, client portals, automated data pulls, monitored uptime and health. The CRM and daedalus work exists to make that a productized part of every retainer rather than a favour.

This is the rate justification. It is also the least-marketed thing the studio does — no page currently sells it.

M5Stated

What is the smallest engagement you will still say yes to?

Becomes the disqualifier line in the plan — the point below which a page should actively repel rather than convert.

There is no single figure. There are three tiers, and the thing that stays fixed across all of them is the rate:

  • ~$500 · small work for people I know. A straightforward business site where the scope is genuinely easy. Not a discount — the rate is the rate, and every minute at the keyboard is billed. These stay welcome.
  • $800 · the absolute minimum for anything else — and only when there is follow-on work behind it.
  • $1,200–$1,600 · a WordPress business site. $2,200 · a modern Next.js build. The real entry price for a new relationship with nothing behind it.

Priced the way DQS-001 was: a deposit at kickoff against a named deliverable, every line visible. Fair and transparent, not negotiated down.

M5aDerived

So what is the page actually supposed to refuse?

The follow-up that turns a price into a filter. Most sites get this wrong by publishing a minimum, which repels the wrong half of the market.

Not small projects — discounted ones. A $500 build at the $80/hr floor is roughly six clean hours; it is perfectly consistent with M2. What gets refused is a rate that moves, and a scope too vague to bill honestly.

So the disqualifier the plan writes for is not a number on a page. It is two questions a page can ask on your behalf: is the scope clear enough to price, and is there anything behind it. A published minimum would turn away the easy six-hour job that pays at rate while doing nothing to deter the vague $3,000 one that loses money.

M6Stated

Who is the client that pays that way?

The rate floor says what work is worth; this says who is willing to pay it. Together they are what makes a search query "qualified" — neither does it alone.

Someone building a specific digital product: a customer-facing app, a custom internal tool, or a premium website that integrates with other systems and apps — ideally systems the studio also built.

They have a budget. They understand that good development is a premium purchase. They appreciate quality, and they do not haggle over a reasonable estimate or over retainer hours.

Company stage does not filter them. All four segments are live on the books right now and all four fit:

  • Specialty boutique — Artist House
  • Early growth — Mineralife, Zemvelo
  • Mid-stage — DQS
  • Enterprise — GDI

The reason stage does not filter is the forward-deployed posture: the studio blends into whatever team needs it, so the shape of the company matters far less than what it is trying to build and how it treats price.

Source: Karol, 2026-08-30

M6aDerived

How should the content be organized, given who that is?

An audience definition that does not change the site's structure has not been used. Turn the buyer into an information architecture.

Do not segment by company size. The reflex is a page for startups and a page for enterprise. That would split an audience M6 says is unified — boutique through enterprise, all paying for the same posture. Size-based pages would divide the traffic and halve the authority of each.

Segment by what is being built instead — an app, a custom tool, a premium website with integrations. That matches the vocabulary the five briefs in Q1 actually used, and it matches the W1 ordering. One axis, used consistently.

The highest-value page is the combined offer: the website and the system it talks to, built by the same person. That is the one version of the website line that cannot be commoditized, and it is the mechanism behind every escalation in Q1 — a site becomes a CRM, a portal, an integration layer.

One thing search cannot do. No query separates a buyer who haggles from one who does not — budget posture is invisible in a keyword. That filter has to live in the copy, in the absence of published prices, and in the two qualifying questions from M5a. The plan can bring the right topic; only the page can turn away the wrong buyer.

W

The work

The service lines, ranked by what they earn rather than by how much of them there is. This ordering becomes the priority order of the content plan.

W1Stated

List what you sell. Rank by margin, not by volume.

Most sites are organized by what the business does most of. The plan should be organized by what pays best.

  • 1 · Fractional retainer — the anchor offer, the thing to be found for.
  • 2 · Web apps & custom tools — the Artist House pattern; highest-value project work.
  • 3 · High-end websites — motion work, AI-driven scroll experiences, multisite builds. Not the commodity business site (that is the funding tier in M5); the version worth building well and charging for.
  • 4 · Automations & integrations — still sold, still real work: Zapier builds, VPS and cloud integration layers, the migration off per-task pricing onto flat-cost infrastructure.
  • 5 · Multi-brand consolidation — specialty, pattern proven twice, public once DQS signs. Closely related to the multisite work in tier 3.

The ordering is a preference as much as a margin calculation, and it was stated that way: building a better, more expensive website beats running someone's Zapiers — without giving up the Zapier work.

Source: Karol, 2026-08-30 · supersedes the strategy doc §3 ordering

W2Stated

For each line: is it what you want to be found for, or what pays for the thing you want?

Separates the hero lines from the funding lines. Both need pages; only one needs a content engine behind it.

Found for: the retainer, custom tools, high-end websites — the genuinely cool ones — and the reporting layer (M4). These get the content engine.

Pays for it: the small business site at the lower M5 tiers, and integration work. Both stay on the menu and both need pages that convert the traffic already arriving — neither needs forty more posts.

The split runs through the websites line rather than around it, which is the part that changes the plan: the same service is a hero offer at one end and a funding line at the other, so it needs two different pages, not one. The dividing line is the buyer's expectation — $2,000–3,000 is the hero tier; $500 is the favour tier.

And the hero tier is not only a margin line. It is the acquisition path: the client who pays $3,000 for a site is the client who later wants the portal, the integrations and the monitoring. Content aimed at that buyer is doing double duty, which is why it outranks its position in W1.

W3Decision

What is the entry offer — the small, obvious thing that becomes the big thing?

The highest-converting content targets the entry offer, not the anchor. Nobody searches for a retainer.

The scoped project with a defined finish line. The sharpest named version is the economics trigger: every scaling company hits the month its Zapier or Workato bill outgrows a server bill, and migrating high-volume automations onto flat-cost infrastructure is a clean, priceable project that converts naturally into a retainer.

That is a search-shaped pain — someone types it the week the invoice lands.

There is now a second candidate, and it is probably the better one. W4 describes the premium website build as automated, high-margin, priced where the buyer already expects to pay, and the natural first step into portals, integrations and monitoring. That is the definition of an entry offer — and unlike the iPaaS trigger, people search for it constantly.

The iPaaS wedge stays sharp: it is a specific pain someone types the week the invoice lands, and it converts straight to a retainer. But it is a narrow door into a service line that ranks fourth by preference.

Q1 settles most of this. Three of the last five clients entered through a website; none entered through an automation bill. And the volume argument is yours: far more people need a website than need a custom app or a complex integration, which makes it the easiest thing to enter with.

Needs Karol: confirmation, not deliberation. The recommendation is to lead with the premium website build and keep the iPaaS migration as a named secondary wedge on the systems page. Say yes and the content plan has its spine.

W4Stated

What would you stop selling tomorrow if the pipeline were full?

The clearest signal of what to stop writing for. An answer here saves more effort than any keyword.

Nothing. No service line gets dropped — the filter is the rate, not the type of work. Website building in particular is now heavily automated and largely hands-off, which changes its economics entirely: at $100–120/hr on a build that takes around eighteen hours, it clears the floor comfortably and is worth taking.

The distinction is the client, not the service. The target is the buyer who expects to pay $2,000–3,000 for a site — not the one shopping at $500. And that buyer is the one who escalates: a site, then a portal, then integrations, then optimizations, then monitoring. The website is the front door to everything in M4.

What gets phased out is work priced below the rate, and the older cheap clients — as new retainers replace the revenue, not before.

W4aFinding

Does the delivered work carry a link back — and is that link actually there?

A credit link on every site shipped is a link-building programme that runs off delivery instead of outreach. It is also the easiest claim in this workbook to believe without checking.

The intent is stated: every site built is tagged, so delivery compounds domain authority and each build keeps working as a business card.

It is not currently running. Four reachable client sites were fetched and searched for any link to tallkarol.com and for any developer credit at all — "built by", "designed by", "developed by". Nothing on any of them:

  • artisthouse.world · no link, no credit
  • dqstaff.com · no link, no credit
  • greatdayimprovements.com · no link, no credit
  • zemvelo.com · no link, no credit

Homepage HTML only, and two other sites were unreachable — so this is a strong signal rather than a complete audit. But the mechanism believed to be compounding authority is not visibly deployed on the four biggest named builds.

Needs Karol: is the credit link something clients have refused, something that quietly stopped, or something never actually implemented? It is close to free, it is the cheapest link acquisition available, and it changes the plan — an existing programme gets measured, an absent one gets built.

X

The exclusions

What must never be optimized for. The highest-value stage in the workbook and the one clients answer fastest — people know what they are not far better than they know what they are.

X1Derived

What do you not do, no matter who asks?

Hard exclusions. The CBD rule: a supplement client who does not sell CBD will still get a research queue full of CBD unless the tool is told.

  • Work below the rate floor, however interesting.
  • Buyers who haggle a reasonable estimate or argue retainer hours down. The behaviour is the disqualifier, not the number — it predicts every later conversation.
  • Engagements structured around an account manager and a ticket queue — the studio's whole pitch is their absence.
  • Positioning as an "AI studio." AI appears only where a specific capability meets a specific noun; "AI-powered solutions" is a banned phrase, not a missed keyword.
  • Publishing prices. Every CTA is a booked call.

X5Derived

What price and status signals must never appear?

Premium positioning is destroyed by vocabulary faster than by design. Collect the banned words now; they become a lint rule.

Never: cheap, affordable, budget, "starting at", discount, hourly rates on any public page — and no published minimum project value either, for the reason in M5a. Never state headcount, never "one-person," never a false "we." "Team" only ever means the client's team.

Also banned as house style: "brands" as a word for the audience, soul, journey, "what's missing," "disconnected" as poetry.

Q2Derived

What happens the week someone decides to go looking for you?

Trigger events are where commercial intent lives. Write for the week of the trigger, not the topic in general.

  • The senior developer resigned and the roadmap has no owner.
  • An agency quote came back at three times the expected number.
  • The automation bill jumped a tier and someone asked why.
  • A launch got blocked by performance or accessibility.
  • An acquisition left the company holding six websites on five hosts.

Confirmed against Q1. The five real briefs add two triggers the list was missing, both more mundane than the dramatic ones above: the site looks dated next to competitors, and a manual process has outgrown the people doing it. Neither is a crisis. Both are the moment someone starts searching.

C

The comparison set

Who the buyer is really choosing between. Competitive research aimed at the wrong set produces a plan to beat companies you never lose to.

C1Stated

Who do you actually lose deals to — by name where you can?

Ask about losses, not competitors. The named loser list is specific; the competitor list is a category.

There is no competitive-bid loss history, because there is barely a competitive-bid pipeline. No named studio, agency or freelancer has taken work off the table. Every client in Q1 arrived through a relationship or escalated out of existing work.

That is not a gap in the record — it is the finding. Acquisition today is referral-shaped, which means the studio has never really been in a market, which is precisely the condition this plan exists to change. It also explains why competitive SERP research has little to bite on yet: there is no incumbent to displace, because there has been no contest.

Consequence for the plan: competitor analysis drops down the priority list for Phase 2. The gap is demand capture, not displacement. Revisit C1 after two quarters of inbound, when losses start to exist and can finally be named.

What can you claim that none of them can?

R3Stated

What number, six months out, would make you say this worked?

Must be countable and must not be traffic. Get the target in writing before the first pull, or every result becomes arguable afterwards.

Answered in capacity, not in leads — which is the better unit, and not the one this question proposed:

  • Floor: 120 billable hours a month.
  • Target: 160–180 hours a month, with overflow allowed to flex into the following month.
  • The gap to close: another 40–60 hours a month. One client, or three.

Retainers preferred, projects accepted. Turning projects into retainers is the aim, but a steady supply of projects is a valid outcome — provided everything is charged for.

Why the unit is right: a lead target is a proxy for capacity. Capacity is the actual constraint, it is the thing that pays, and it does not require guessing a conversion rate the site cannot measure yet.

R3bDerived

What happens if the plan works better than the target?

A capacity target has a ceiling, and a ceiling changes what the plan should optimize for. Ask it before the pipeline fills, not after.

The plan should deliberately aim past 180 hours, and this is the reason: surplus demand is the only honest mechanism for the M2 rate goal. Nothing else moves $80/hr to $120/hr by April 2028. Not a pricing page, not better positioning copy — only being visibly unable to take every job.

That reframes overflow. Demand above capacity is not waste to be avoided; it is the instrument that raises the rate, retires the cheap clients on schedule, and makes the roster scarcity in the strategy doc true rather than theatrical.

It also sets the report's escalation rule: the month the pipeline consistently exceeds 180 hours is the month the floor moves, not the month more content gets commissioned.
